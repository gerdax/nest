Hi Adam,

With a 4-Health rat, the conclusion changes: the current fight is already very forgiving for straightforward attacking. Always attacking wins 97.16%, almost the same as optimal play at 97.24%. The main balancing problem is giving Preparation a useful, understandable role rather than making the rat easier to kill.

This follow-up assumes player Health 10, Agility 3, Composure 3, rat Health 4, and compares starting Energy 3 with 10. Both resource caps stay 10. These are virtual test variants: I have not changed the saved project or published fight; the published snapshot still has an 8-Health rat. Enemy intentions remain hidden and the rat has no stats in every option.

The baseline retains current rules: player Attack tests Agility, player Preparation tests Composure, both at difficulty 3 (62.5% at stat 3); Defend costs 1 Energy and checks fixed 3d6 ≥ 10 only after an incoming hit. Rat attacks/blocks also use fixed 3d6 ≥ 10. Bite deals 2, Charge 4; normal player damage is 2 and the prepared bonus is +2. A landed prepared hit therefore already kills the full-health rat.

Preparation adds only 0.005 percentage points to optimal survival at these starting values. Increasing Composure from 3 to 4 changes the best survival rate from 97.24% to 97.24% when rounded to two decimals (the actual increase is 0.006 points); increasing Agility from 3 to 4 raises it to 99.23%. More Energy alone barely changes the control: optimal survival is 97.239% at Energy 3 versus 97.242% at Energy 10.

  1. Automatic player Preparation: remove its dice roll. Spending the action always grants +2 damage for the next Attack; that Attack still rolls normally and consumes the bonus even on a miss or rat block. Keep all other rules. This is the cleanest way to reduce dice and make the card’s immediate effect predictable. The repeated Prepare/Attack strategy improves from 88.27% to 94.76%, but still trails always attacking at 97.16%. So removing the roll helps usability and reliability, but does not by itself make preparation survival-optimal. Tradeoff: Composure would lose its current combat function unless it gets another role.

  2. Keep the Composure check, but bank its reward until an Attack actually deals damage. A miss or rat block does not consume the prepared bonus; it still does not stack. This removes the frustration of passing one check only to lose its whole reward to the next failed check. Cue-based preparation reaches 97.74%, almost matching the profile optimum 97.75%, while keeping a real setup turn and the risk of retaliation. Composure 3→4 now raises optimal survival to 98.58%, a gain of 0.83 points. Tradeoff: at default Agility 3 / Composure 3, preparing and then attacking until damage lands can become a routine near-optimal pattern. That is not universal: at Agility 4 / Composure 3, always Attack wins 99.20%, versus 98.72% for the Prepare/Attack cycle.

  3. Keep checked Preparation, and add +2 to the next Attack roll as well as +2 damage. This is a temporary roll modifier, not a permanent Agility increase. At Agility 3 it raises that prepared attack’s own hit chance from 62.5% to 83.80%; the rat may still block it, and the bonus is still consumed on the attempt. Cue-based preparation reaches 97.27%, versus 97.16% for always attacking; the profile optimum is 97.45%. Composure 3→4 raises the optimum to 98.12%. This is a smaller, risk-preserving nudge than option 2, with the cost of another modifier that must be shown clearly in the UI. It is not a large survival buff in such a short fight.

  4. Make paid Defend reliably reduce damage by 2, without a player defense roll. It still costs 1 Energy and the turn, expires after the next enemy attack including a miss, and is wasted against a rat block as today. Rat defense remains dice-checked. A hit from Bite deals 0; a hit from Charge still deals 2, so this is not guaranteed full safety against a charge. Optimal survival is 97.41%; cue-based defense reaches 96.91%, still below always Attack at 97.16%. This direction simplifies the defensive experience and reduces the worst damage spike, but it does not fix Preparation or Composure’s weak payoff.

  5. Rescale damage so 4 Health contains more exchanges. Test player basic damage 1, checked Preparation +3 (prepared damage 4), rat Bite 1 and Charge 2. All rolls and costs remain unchanged. This is a broader redesign: normal attacks need four hits, while a prepared hit can still finish the rat. Optimal survival becomes 99.68%, and always attacking already wins 99.60%. The fight is longer and much harder to lose. Preparation can then save actions and retain Health even if it does not maximize survival, as the table below shows. Tradeoff: this risks making the tutorial grindy or nearly unlosable; it should not be adopted just to equalize action win rates.

My recommendation. Keep the 4-Health rat as the introductory baseline; do not lower its damage solely to improve win rate. For the next prototype, I would test option 2 if you want passing Preparation to feel worthwhile and preserve Composure, or option 3 if you want a lighter change that retains more risk. Option 1 is a sensible simpler-UX direction, but needs a deliberate decision about Composure’s role. Option 4 is a separate defense-design decision; option 5 is a campaign-wide damage-scale experiment. These are alternatives, not a proposal to stack every buff.

Simply increasing the current prepared damage above 4 would not help this particular rat: the landed prepared hit is already lethal. Once one direction is chosen, test it against tougher enemies and sequences of encounters too. A first enemy cannot establish the whole combat system’s balance.

Verification and limits. I compared all six profiles using a numerical model with certified lower/upper convergence, plus 2,640,000 independent actual-dice counterfactual simulations. Another 240,000 fights used the production coordinator with temporary in-memory data overrides for the control, automatic Preparation and rescaled-damage profiles. Those three require only authored data changes; the other three are model-only mechanics and would need implementation/playtesting. The largest model-simulation discrepancy was 2.55 standard errors, and 1.87 for production checks. The attached CSV summarizes 36 full-Health profile/stat/Energy cases, including the stat sensitivities.

No physical device, animation, UI comprehension or real beginner testing is established by these numbers. The optimal policies optimize survival, not pace or future resource value; random and fixed policies are not measurements of actual beginners. Initial Health matters: the control’s optimum drops to 92.02% if the player enters at Health 8 rather than 10.
